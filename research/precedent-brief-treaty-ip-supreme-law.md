# PRECEDENT BRIEF
## Grounding Indigenous Intellectual-Property & Cultural Protection in Treaties as "the supreme Law of the Land"
**U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2 · Federal Indian Law Canon · 2025–2026 Developments**
*Research current as of August 30, 2026. Items that could not be independently verified are flagged ⚠.*

---

## THESIS (one paragraph)

The Supremacy Clause makes treaties "the supreme Law of the Land," and the Supreme Court has held for nearly two centuries that treaties with Indian nations are *self-executing* federal law that state law must yield to — *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832). Unlike the foreign-relations treaties at issue in *Medellín v. Texas*, 552 U.S. 491 (2008), Indian treaties have always been enforced directly by courts without implementing legislation: they *reserve*, rather than grant, rights — *United States v. Winans*, 198 U.S. 371 (1905) — including usufructuary rights to fish, hunt, and gather that courts have protected into the twenty-first century (*Minnesota v. Mille Lacs Band*, 526 U.S. 172 (1999); *United States v. Michigan*, 471 F. Supp. 192 (W.D. Mich. 1979)). **No reported federal decision yet extends this reserved-rights, supreme-law reasoning to intangible cultural expressions or traditional knowledge as intellectual property** (see Part C) — that step is untested, "pushing precedent." This brief assembles the authority that supports it and marks what is unverified.

## PART C — CULTURAL PROPERTY VIA TREATY: DIRECT PRECEDENT OR UNTESTED?

**Direct precedent: NONE — this is an untested, "pushing precedent" theory.** After searching reported federal decisions, law reviews, and 2025–26 dockets, **no U.S. court has held that a treaty with an Indian nation creates enforceable rights in intangible cultural expressions, traditional knowledge, or other intangible heritage as intellectual property.** State this explicitly in any brief: *there is no on-point case*; the argument must be built by analogy and by statutory reinforcement.

**What the law actually protects (the building blocks):**
1. **Treaty-reserved usufructuary rights are enforceable, property-like, supreme-law rights.** *Winans* (1905) ("a reservation of those not granted"); *Winters* (1908); *Menominee Tribe* (1968); *Mille Lacs* (1999); *United States v. Michigan* (1979) and *United States v. Washington* (1974). These cases enforce treaty reservations of *practices tied to lands and waters* (fishing, hunting, gathering) — the closest doctrinal analogue to reserving cultural practices, and they show Indian treaties are self-executing despite *Medellín*.
2. **Treaty text + the reserved-rights canon.** Because treaties "reserve, not grant," unextinguished cultural rights survive unless Congress clearly abrogates them (*Dion*, 476 U.S. 734 (1986)); abrogation is a high bar. Note the counterweight: *Lone Wolf* (1903) plenary power — never overruled.
3. **Supremacy-Clause preemption of conflicting state law.** *Worcester*; applied to state fisheries regulation in Michigan and Washington. The same preemptive force would operate against state IP/trade-secret/unfair-competition regimes if a treaty right were recognized.
4. **Statutory layers (not treaty-based, but complementary).** NAGPRA (tangible cultural items; 2024 rule's deference to tribal knowledge and duty of care); IACA (market-protection for Indian-produced goods; civil/criminal); NHPA/AIPA (cultural landscapes). Sacred-site claims have so far failed on **statute/Free-Exercise** grounds — *Lyng* (1988); *Apache Stronghold* (cert. denied 2025) — precisely because no treaty theory was litigated; those losses do not foreclose a treaty-based claim.
5. **Tribal-law innovation.** Tribal courts are moving on rights-of-nature/cultural-protection (e.g., the White Earth manoomin "Rights of Nature" line) — tribal law, not treaty, but it shows doctrinal receptivity.
6. **Foreign analogues (persuasive only).** Australia: *Bulun Bulun v. R & T Textiles Pty Ltd* (1998) 157 ALR 193 (FCA) (equitable relief for communal/customary ownership of artistic expression); Canada: *R. v. Van der Peet* [1996] 2 S.C.R. 507 (s. 35 aboriginal rights to practices integral to distinctive culture); New Zealand: Wai 262 (Waitangi Tribunal; mātauranga Māori / traditional knowledge as Treaty of Waitangi interest).
7. **Scholarship supporting the push.** Angela R. Riley & Kristen A. Carpenter, *Owning Red: A Theory of Indian (Cultural) Appropriation*, 94 Tex. L. Rev. 859 (2016); Rebecca Tsosie, *Reclaiming Native Stories: An Essay on Cultural Appropriation and Cultural Rights*, 32 Ariz. St. L.J. 299 (2000) ⚠ volume/pin unverified; Trevor G. Reed, *Creative Sovereignties: Should Copyright Apply on Tribal Lands?*, J. Copyright Soc'y USA (2024/25) ⚠ volume/pages unverified; MSU ILPC Turtle Talk blog, "intangible cultural property" archive.

**Bottom line for strategy:** (i) plead the treaty-reservation theory directly (self-executing treaty + reserved-rights canon + Supremacy Clause), (ii) use NAGPRA/IACA as evidence of Congress's consistent protective intent, (iii) anchor to the Michigan/Washington usufructuary line as the controlling analogue, and (iv) be candid that **no court has yet recognized intangible cultural expressions as treaty-reserved property** — the honest framing converts the absence of precedent from a weakness into the "pushing precedent" argument this brief is designed to support.
