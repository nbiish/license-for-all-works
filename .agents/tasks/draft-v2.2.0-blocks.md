## BLOCK 1 — Insert after TREATY RIGHTS FOUNDATION items (line ~33 area)

**TREATIES AS SUPREME LAW AND INTERNATIONAL OBLIGATIONS**

The treaty rights invoked by this license carry the full force of the "supreme Law of the Land"
under U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2. It is long-settled that ratified treaties stand on the highest
plane of federal law and displace conflicting state law: *Foster & Elam v. Neilson*, 27 U.S. 253
(1829) (treaties "constitute the supreme law of the land"); *Whitney v. Robertson*, 124 U.S. 190
(1888) (later-in-time treaty controls over conflicting state statutes); *Missouri v. Holland*,
252 U.S. 416 (1920) (valid treaties bind states regardless of contrary state policy, with
Congressional implementation). To the extent a treaty provision requires implementing legislation
for a particular private cause of action (*Medellín v. Texas*, 552 U.S. 491 (2008) —
non-self-execution doctrine), the rights asserted here do not depend on that pathway: they arise
independently as (i) contractual obligations accepted by every user upon access; (ii) exercises of
inherent tribal sovereignty and tribal law (Section 7.1A, Theory 7); and (iii) federal statutory
protections (IACA, NAGPRA, Copyright Act). The non-self-execution caveat therefore narrows nothing.

Internationally, the 1836 and 1855 treaties are nation-to-nation instruments whose observance is a
measured of the United States' good-faith international standing: UNDRIP Article 37 affirms
"the right to the recognition, observance and enforcement of treaties" concluded with Indigenous
peoples; UNDRIP Articles 8(2), 11 and 31 (endorsed by the United States, Dec. 2010) affirm
redress and effective mechanisms against cultural-property deprivation; and the United States'
ratification of the UNESCO 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural
Heritage (deposit Dec. 2023) marks its first binding cultural-heritage convention — reinforcing,
not replacing, the treaty-reserved rights framework here. [PENDING VERIFICATION: UNESCO date/mechanism]
